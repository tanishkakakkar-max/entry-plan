# Hero Cosmetics → Japan

**Brand:** Hero Cosmetics - hydrocolloid acne patches (Mighty Patch) plus a small acne-care range. Friendly, science-backed, fun. Sold in the US through Amazon, Target, Walmart, Ulta and DTC. Owned by Church & Dwight since its $630 million acquisition in 2022 [W1]; already in Canada (its first international market, 2023 [W2]), Australia and Greece, so this would not be a first international step.
**Customer today:** US teens and twenty-somethings who treat a breakout as a normal, fixable thing.
**Target market:** Japan

---

## 1. The call
**Test first, through a Japanese beauty distributor.** Japan already understands the product: hydrocolloid pimple patches are an established shelf item, mostly sold as general goods at ¥15-30 a patch [W3], and Korean beauty is the fastest-growing import story in the country [W4]. That is the problem as much as the opening. Mighty Patch is made in Korea [W5] and priced like an American brand, so in Japan it would be a US label selling a Korean product at two to three times the local price. The way in is a distributor that already places foreign beauty into Japanese lifestyle stores and drugstores, a Japan-specific pack and price, and a clear read on how Japan classifies the product before a single unit ships.

## 2. Recommended entry point
- **City:** Tokyo, where lifestyle retail and beauty media concentrate.
- **Channel:** one importer-distributor with existing accounts at lifestyle stores and drugstores (the Loft and Matsumoto Kiyoshi tier, which already stock Korean beauty [W4]), plus Qoo10 Japan and Amazon.co.jp for discovery. No standalone DTC in year one.
- **Customer:** women 18-29 who already buy Korean skincare and read reviews before trying anything new.
- **Price:** set in yen from landed cost and the local per-patch benchmark. A Japan-only pack (fewer, mixed-size patches) is the likely answer to landing near the ¥15-30 per patch range [W3] without discounting the US brand [verify].

## 3. Entry strategy
**First 90 days (classification and partner)**
- Get a written classification opinion. Most acne patches in Japan are sold as general goods, while at least one domestic patch is listed as a Class I general medical device [W3]. If any Hero claim or formulation pushes it into cosmetics or quasi-drugs, the PMD Act requires a Japanese marketing authorization holder, Japanese labels and a prefectural marketing notification before import [W6] [verify].
- Shortlist two distributors who have launched a US or Korean skincare brand into Japanese lifestyle retail in the last three years. Ask for sell-through, not store counts.

**Months 3-9 (one retail cluster, one platform)**
- Launch in a single lifestyle-store chain in Tokyo with a small acne-care set (Original, Invisible+, one balm) and on Qoo10 Japan, where Korean skincare already sells [W4].
- Seed 20 Japanese skincare creators on Instagram and YouTube with the peel-off content that built the brand in the US. Japanese shoppers research before they buy, and reviews on @cosme-style platforms and marketplaces carry more weight than ads.
- Translate the "science-backed" story into evidence: the brand's own hydrocolloid testing plus the published pilot trial that found faster reduction in redness and severity with hydrocolloid dressings than with plain tape, in a sample of only 20 patients [W7]. Say what the evidence is and is not.

**Months 9-18 (drugstore decision)**
- If the lifestyle cluster sells through and reviews hold, take the distributor's drugstore relationships to one national chain. If price is the barrier the data shows, keep Japan as a premium niche and stop there.

## 4. Consumer psychology
- **The global-brand halo is real, and it is Hero's only pricing argument.** Japanese consumers valued an American fast-food brand mainly as a global icon and discounted its localization efforts [W8]. Hero should sell as "the number one patch in America," not pretend to be local.
- **Korea sets the quality benchmark in this category.** Korean lipstick took 55% of Japan's lipstick imports last year and Korean brands are moving into Loft and Matsumoto Kiyoshi because shoppers want to see them in person [W4]. A Korean-made patch is credible; the US price is not, unless the pack and claims justify it.
- **Value-consciousness is rising with inflation.** Shoppers are judging new price levels carefully, so the per-patch price will be compared.
- **Fun must be quiet.** Hero's playful US tone works as friendliness; loud empowerment copy and superlatives read as untrustworthy here.
- **Inspection culture applies to packaging.** A patch sheet that peels badly or a box that dents in transit will show up in reviews within a week.

## 5. Brand adaptation
- **Keep:** Mighty Patch Original and Invisible+, the hydrocolloid-first story, the friendly tone, the peel-off content format.
- **Change:** Japanese-language packaging and leaflets; a smaller mixed pack for the local price point; the "science-backed" claim rewritten as plain evidence; claims pre-checked against the product's Japanese classification [W6].
- **Add:** a limited design edition with a Japanese character or artist, which the original CAVA Japan research flagged as a shortcut to feeling local for Gen Z.
- **Drop:** US retailer logos, "#1" claims not backed by Japanese data, and any medical-sounding promise.

## 6. Competitive reality
- **Domestic general-goods patches:** cheap, everywhere, sold on Amazon and Rakuten at roughly ¥15-30 a patch, with at least one registered as a Class I medical device [W3]. They set the price floor.
- **Korean skincare brands:** the premium reference for this category, already stocked at Loft and Matsumoto Kiyoshi and growing fast on Qoo10 Japan [W4].
- **Domestic acne skincare:** ingredient-literate shoppers and strong local brands make "acne care" a crowded shelf; Hero should compete only on the patch.

## 7. Risks
| Risk | Type | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wrong product classification | Regulatory | Medium | High | Written opinion before import; if cosmetic or quasi-drug, appoint a Japanese marketing authorization holder and file the prefectural notification [W6] [verify] |
| Priced out by domestic and Korean patches | Cost | High | High | Japan-only pack near the local per-patch band [W3]; distributor pricing built from landed cost |
| Distributor treats Hero as one more SKU | Operational | Medium | High | Sell-through targets and marketing commitments in the contract; one chain at a time |
| Claims trigger complaints | Regulatory | Medium | Medium | Only claims allowed for the product's category; evidence stated with its limits [W7] |
| Yen weakness lifts landed cost | Cost | Medium | Medium | Price in yen from landed cost with a buffer; re-quote quarterly |
| Reviews punish packaging or adhesion issues | Cultural | Medium | Medium | Japan-standard QC on sheets and boxes before the first shipment |

## 8. Rewards
- A Japanese lifestyle-retail listing is the strongest beauty credential in Asia and travels to Korea and Taiwan conversations.
- Japan forces discipline on pack size, claims and evidence that will make Hero sharper everywhere else.
- Church & Dwight already reports Hero as a driver of international growth [W9]; Japan is the market that proves whether that growth is premium or just distribution.

## 9. Validate before you spend
1. Buy and test 10 patches sold in Japan (domestic and Korean); record price per patch, adhesion and packaging quality.
2. Get the classification opinion from a Japanese regulatory consultant [verify].
3. Interview two distributors who have placed a foreign skincare brand in Loft-tier stores; ask for 12-month sell-through data.
4. Run a Japanese-language creator seed on Instagram with 20 creators and measure saves and comments before any retail commitment.
5. Model landed cost and the Japan-only pack at three price points.

**Confidence:** Medium. The category and channel are proven; the price gap and the classification question decide everything, and both are checkable in 90 days.
